Swipe, message, meet for coffee, repeat.

For a short while, this can feel exhilarating. Novelty activates the brain's dopamine systems. Meeting someone new can feel exciting, hopeful, even electric.

But novelty comes with a cost. Every new person requires your nervous system to run a series of rapid assessments:

- Is this person safe?
- Do I like them?
- Do they like me?
- How should I present myself?
- What parts of my life do I share?

That constant evaluation creates what the glossary calls **interaction uncertainty**. Your system stays slightly activated, because every encounter begins from zero. Over time, this produces what many people now describe as dating fatigue.

The fatigue is not simply emotional disappointment. It is also cognitive and physiological load. The brain is repeatedly processing unfamiliar people, unfamiliar dynamics, and unfamiliar outcomes. It is one reason so many people feel drained after long periods of active dating, whether through apps, speed dating, or a run of first dates.

## This is new

What is striking is how recent this pattern is.

For most of human existence, relationships formed through repeated exposure in shared environments. People met through villages, neighbourhoods, workplaces, religious spaces, markets, and community gathering places. The same people appeared again and again. Familiarity grew naturally, without anyone engineering it.

Psychologist **Robert Zajonc** described what he called the *mere exposure effect*: simply seeing someone repeatedly increases our sense of comfort and positive regard toward them. Familiarity itself creates the conditions for connection.

Sociologist **Ray Oldenburg** described the places that support this as *third places*. First place is home. Second place is work. Third places are the informal social environments where community forms on its own.

These environments allow what sociologist **Mark Granovetter** called *weak ties* to develop. People see each other repeatedly in low-pressure contexts. Over time, acquaintances can gradually become friendships or relationships. There is no performance required and no formal dating structure — just familiarity slowly building.

## Continuity

This helps explain something many people quietly notice. Some of the strongest attractions and relationships do not come from apps or events. They appear in places where we simply see someone regularly.

A colleague. Someone in the same yoga class. Someone at the gym. Someone who always walks their dog at the same time.

Nobody is trying to date in those moments. But the nervous system registers something important: **continuity**. The environment becomes predictable. Safety increases. Curiosity replaces evaluation. Connection can grow more organically.

This is not an argument for dating your coworkers, or for treating every environment you frequent as a hunting ground. Boundaries still matter, and they matter more in the places you cannot easily leave.

But it does say something about modern dating fatigue. If every attempt at connection happens in a high-novelty environment, the nervous system never gets the chance to relax into familiarity. You keep starting from zero, and you pay for it in load.

## A different move

Sometimes the more sustainable approach is not to put yourself out there more aggressively, but to build a life with repeated environments — places where you show up regularly. The same class, the same gym, the same coffee shop, the same park.

These create what the glossary calls **relational density**: the simple experience of encountering the same people again and again. From a nervous-system perspective, familiarity reduces vigilance. Social interaction gets lighter. Small conversations accumulate.

And sometimes, in those environments, something unexpected happens. Instead of forcing connection, you simply recognise it.
