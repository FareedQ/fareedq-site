## What it means

Minimal Viable Life is the smallest sufficiently sustainable and repeatable arrangement of practical supports, obligations, and opportunities that enables a person to function while preserving room for growth and fulfilment.

Both words are doing work. *Minimal* means sufficient — not austere, not deprived, not reduced to crisis survival. *Viable* means workable in a real life, not merely elegant on paper. A structure that only holds on a good week, or that requires constant discipline to maintain, is not viable by this standard however impressive it looks.

It is also assessed across an ordinary week or season rather than against a perfect day. Some weeks are maintenance-only. That is not failure; it is what a workable structure does under load.

## Where the idea comes from

The concept is shaped by my former career in software engineering, and by the startup idea of a Minimum Viable Product. Instead of spending years imagining the ideal product, development begins with a workable version, observes what real use reveals, and iterates from there.

Applied to a life, the lesson is that plans and routines should be tested against lived experience rather than elaborated indefinitely before participation begins. You find out what a week can hold by living one, not by designing one.

The analogy has a limit worth stating. A human life is not a product, and personal worth or enjoyment should never be reduced to an optimisation metric. The useful part of the idea is the iteration. The dangerous part would be treating a person as something to be efficiently specified.

## Three layers

A viable life holds three kinds of things, and they compete for the same finite week.

{{layers}}

The middle layer is where most people get stuck, and it is worth being concrete about why. If everything is designated an expansion project, demands become excessive and nothing gets maintained well. If maintenance expands until it occupies all available space, life becomes functional and stagnant. The practical move is to choose a small number of growth tracks deliberately, and to let the rest of your life sit at a sustainable maintenance level without treating that as a compromise.

The top layer is the one most often sacrificed first, because it is the only layer that produces nothing. That is exactly why it needs protecting in advance rather than being left to whatever remains.

## The 4–2–1 orientation, and why it is optional

An organising prompt that some people find useful is four, two, one: roughly four grounding anchors that protect the foundation, up to two active expansion priorities, and at least one meaningful opening for unstructured enjoyment during the week.

It is a prompt for distributing attention, not a validated formula, a fixed ratio, or a mandated number of activities. Anyone who has had a season of illness, grief, financial pressure, or heavy study knows that the ratio has to change. Those seasons call for fewer expansion tracks and more recovery. A more stable season can carry more ambition. The more durable instruction is simpler: build for a good week, not a perfect day.

## How it is practised

The cycle is short and unglamorous. Identify what is indispensable. Separate maintenance from genuine expansion. Protect unstructured time before something else claims it. Test the structure in an actual week. Revise it based on where the strain and the satisfaction actually showed up.

The presence of an efficient routine is not evidence that any of this is working. Plenty of people run an impressive schedule that leaves no room to be in their own life. The test is whether the structure lets you live with reasonable continuity and freedom — not whether the management of life has become its own absorbing project.

## What it is not

- **Not minimalism.** The framework is not asking you to own less, do less, or want less. It is asking what enough actually is, for you, in your circumstances.
- **Not a productivity system.** A viable life is not organised to maximise output. It is organised so that output is not the only thing it can produce.
- **Not an argument for a smaller life.** Low capacity is what makes a life feel small: narrow, obligatory, and short on desire. Building capacity is what makes a larger life possible without carrying it on strain.
- **Not a fair fight.** Circumstances and support differ enormously. Someone caring for a parent, working two jobs, or managing an illness is not failing at life design; they are working with different constraints. A framework that turned unequal access into individual blame would be worse than useless.
