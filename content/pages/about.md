## Fifteen years of systems

For more than fifteen years I worked as a software engineer, building large-scale digital systems alongside people I respected. I contributed to products and infrastructure at companies including Meta Platforms and Loblaws Digital, working across mobile platforms, augmented reality infrastructure, and machine learning tools.

Technology trains you to think in systems. You learn how small structural decisions influence behaviour at scale. You learn how feedback loops form, and how the architecture of a system quietly shapes the outcomes it produces, often more than any individual decision inside it.

Much of that work happened in product organisations built around ambition and speed, and several of those years were spent living in New York City. Environments like that teach you a great deal about building things. They also teach you something less comfortable about how easily chronic urgency starts to feel like a normal way to live — and how much of a life can be postponed in the name of getting somewhere.

## Noticing the wrong kind of problem

Over time I began to notice that the problems that mattered most to the people around me were not technical ones.

Friends and colleagues were navigating stress, burnout, relationships, identity, and meaning. Some of the most outwardly successful people I knew were privately stuck on questions that no productivity system, no better tool, and no amount of discipline had touched. The more closely I looked, the clearer it became that the systems shaping our lives were not only digital. They were biological, psychological, relational, and practical — and they were largely invisible to the way we usually talk about change.

## A turn

Eventually I stepped away from the technology industry and took time to travel, reflect, and rebuild my own health. That period became the turning point. I began studying the body, nervous-system regulation, and psychology — initially out of personal curiosity, and gradually with professional intention.

What I found was a second kind of systems thinking. The nervous system is an architecture too. It decides, below conscious awareness, what is safe, what is possible, and what is worth wanting. Many of the struggles people bring to coaching are not failures of willpower or character. They are usually the predictable output of a system running under chronic stress, relational uncertainty, or an environment that quietly erodes capacity. Understand the system and you gain some ability to change how you participate in it. That idea is the whole basis of my work.

## Where I am now

That exploration led me back into formal education, and I want to be precise about where that stands.

- I am a **Registered Massage Therapy student at Conestoga College**, studying body-based therapeutic practice.
- I am a **psychology student at the University of Guelph**, studying psychological frameworks and how they interact with the body.
- I volunteer with the **Canadian Mental Health Association**, supporting people through grounded conversation, emotional support, and practical regulation strategies.
- I have completed **trauma-informed training through Wilfrid Laurier University**, which shapes how I approach safety, care, and pace in this work.
- I have studied somatic and nervous-system approaches, including Somatic Experiencing and polyvagal theory, and I continue to study them.
- My long-term goal is to continue deepening this work and to pursue designation as a **Registered Psychotherapist**.

I am transparent about this because it matters. I am not a psychotherapist, I am not a Somatic Experiencing practitioner, and I do not present myself as either. My coaching practice is built on what I have trained in, studied, and practised — nervous-system awareness, somatic principles, systems thinking, and structured conversation — and it stays carefully inside that scope.

## What I actually do

Through coaching, conversation, and education, I help people develop awareness of their own nervous system, build a workable structure for their life, and practise participating in it rather than only preparing for it.

That is what I mean by life coaching: a working relationship over time, aimed at how you actually live rather than at how well you manage. I currently work one-to-one with a small number of [pilot clients](/work-with-me/), and the practice is deliberately small while I refine how this work is done.

I care about dignity more than motivation, and about capacity more than hustle. Growth that has to be forced is usually growth that will not hold. When people understand their own system, they stop needing to be pushed — the direction becomes theirs.

The framework I work from, **Authentic Manifestation**, exists because the parts kept needing to be held together. Regulation without a workable life structure turns into self-improvement that has nowhere to land. A well-designed life without the willingness to participate becomes an impressive container for nothing. And participation without the other two tends to be attempted from a place of depletion, which is why it so often collapses into another obligation.

The **Minimal Viable Life** pillar comes directly out of the technology years: it borrows the iterative logic of a minimum viable product — build something workable, see what real use reveals, revise — while refusing to treat a person as a product to be optimised.

## Where the ideas come from

Almost nothing in my framework is mine by origin. The scientific foundation belongs to researchers and clinicians who have spent their lives on this: Stephen Porges and polyvagal theory, Peter Levine and Somatic Experiencing, and the writing of Bessel van der Kolk and Gabor Maté, along with the many practitioners who translated that work into something usable. Those influences do not carry equal evidentiary weight, and I try not to flatten that.

My contribution is the integration and the application — translating nervous-system science into the ordinary decisions of a life, and placing internal capacity, external structure, and participation in one frame so that it becomes something a person can actually act on.

Frameworks that pretend to be born in isolation are fragile. Frameworks that acknowledge their lineage are stronger.

## Other Projects

The [Therapeutic Relationship](https://therapeuticrelationship.org) is a separate project exploring the ideas, experiences, and relationships that shape how we connect with ourselves and others.

It is written for people in the caring professions rather than for coaching clients, and it is not a service I offer here. Alongside the writing, it publishes plain-language briefs on the therapeutic alliance: what the research found, how strong the evidence is, and what it does not prove.

## A note on scope

Coaching is personal development support. It is not psychotherapy, diagnosis, medical treatment, trauma therapy, or crisis intervention, and it does not replace them. Where what you bring would be better served by clinical care, I will say so plainly.

The full statement of what coaching does and does not cover is on the [pilot page](/work-with-me/#scope), and it is there deliberately rather than buried.
