## Fifteen years of systems

For more than fifteen years I worked as a software engineer, building large-scale digital systems alongside people I respected. I contributed to products and infrastructure at companies including Meta Platforms and Loblaws Digital, working across mobile platforms, augmented reality infrastructure, and machine learning tools.

Technology trains you to think in systems. You learn how small structural decisions influence behaviour at scale. You learn how feedback loops form, and how the architecture of a system quietly shapes the outcomes it produces, often more than any individual decision inside it.

## Noticing the wrong kind of problem

Over time I began to notice something. The problems that mattered most to the people around me were not technical ones.

Friends and colleagues were navigating stress, burnout, relationships, identity, and meaning. Some of the most outwardly successful people I knew were privately stuck on questions that no productivity system, no better tool, and no amount of discipline had touched. The more closely I looked, the clearer it became that the systems shaping our lives were not only digital. They were biological, psychological, and relational — and they were largely invisible to the way we usually talk about change.

## A turn

Eventually I stepped away from the technology industry and took time to travel, reflect, and rebuild my own health. That period became the turning point. I began studying the body, nervous-system regulation, and psychology — initially out of personal curiosity, and gradually with professional intention.

What I found was a second kind of systems thinking. The nervous system is an architecture too. It decides, below conscious awareness, what is safe, what is possible, and what is worth wanting. Many of the struggles people bring to coaching are not failures of willpower or character. They are usually the predictable output of a system running under chronic stress, relational uncertainty, or an environment that quietly erodes capacity. Understand the system and you gain the ability to change how you participate in it. That idea is the whole basis of my work.

## Where I am now

That exploration led me back into formal education, and I want to be precise about where that stands.

- I am a **Registered Massage Therapy student at Conestoga College**, studying body-based therapeutic practice.
- I am a **psychology student at the University of Guelph**, studying psychological frameworks and how they interact with the body.
- I volunteer with the **Canadian Mental Health Association**, supporting people through grounded conversation, emotional support, and practical regulation strategies.
- I have completed **trauma-informed training through Wilfrid Laurier University**, which shapes how I approach safety, care, and pace in this work.
- My long-term goal is to continue deepening this work and to pursue designation as a **Registered Psychotherapist**.

I am transparent about this because it matters. I am not a psychotherapist, and I do not present myself as one. My coaching practice is built on what I have trained in, studied, and practised — nervous-system awareness, somatic principles, systems thinking, and structured conversation — and it stays carefully inside that scope.

## What I actually do

Through somatic coaching, conversation, and education, I help people develop awareness of their own nervous system, strengthen their capacity for connection, and build lives that feel both regulated and alive.

I care about dignity more than motivation, and about capacity more than hustle. Growth that has to be forced is usually growth that will not hold. When people understand their own system, they stop needing to be pushed — the direction becomes theirs.

## Where the ideas come from

Almost nothing in my framework is mine by origin. The scientific foundation belongs to researchers and clinicians who have spent their lives on this: Stephen Porges and polyvagal theory, Peter Levine and Somatic Experiencing, and the many practitioners who translated that work into something usable. My contribution is the integration and the application — translating nervous-system science into the ordinary decisions of a life, and building structure around regulation, capacity, and authorship so that it becomes something you can actually act on.

Frameworks that pretend to be born in isolation are fragile. Frameworks that acknowledge their lineage are stronger.
